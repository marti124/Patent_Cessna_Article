Annotated Bibliography

Sources for the essay,
“Jefferson’s Bargain: When Patents Expire, Anyone May Build. The 1969 Betrayal”

Every source behind the essay, verified against the United States Reports, the Federal Register, the FAA, the patent record and the founding documents, each one linked.

By Roleigh Martin

The essay carries eleven endnotes, and several of them are dense — a type certificate data sheet, four Federal Register citations in a row, a Part 23 amendment list. A reader who meets “70 Fed. Reg. 2561” cold has no way to know whether it matters.

So each entry here opens with a plain sentence saying what the source establishes. The formal citation follows, and then a live link to the document itself. Nothing is asked of the reader on faith.

Footnotes & Sources

Grouped under the essay’s eleven endnotes, in the order the essay cites them. Where one endnote carries several sources, each source is given its own entry beneath that endnote’s heading.

Endnote 1
Jefferson to McPherson, 1813
The letter the essay opens with. Writing to a Baltimore miller, Jefferson says an idea spreads like fire, that one who lights a taper at another’s receives light without darkening it, that inventions cannot in nature be a subject of property, and that society may give an exclusive right as an encouragement. Every quotation in the essay’s first paragraph comes from this one letter.
Thomas Jefferson to Isaac McPherson, August 13, 1813. Founders Online, National Archives.

Endnote 2
Statute of Monopolies, 1624
The English ancestor of the Patent Clause. Parliament abolished royal monopolies, then carved out one exception for new inventions and put a clock on it: fourteen years or under. The principle that an inventor’s exclusive right must end did not begin in Philadelphia.
Statute of Monopolies, 21 Jac. I, c. 3, § 6. Royal assent May 29, 1624; legislation.gov.uk dates the parliamentary session 1623.
View source →
Farrand, Records of the Federal Convention
The Convention’s own record of where the limit came from. Madison’s proposal of August 18, 1787 would secure authors for a limited time. That phrase became the Clause’s “limited Times.”
Max Farrand, ed., The Records of the Federal Convention of 1787, vol. 2 (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1911), entry for August 18, 1787. Law Library of Congress scan of the complete volume. The link opens the whole of volume 2 as a PDF; go to the entry dated August 18, 1787.
View source →
Madison’s Detached Memoranda, ca. 1820
Madison, more than thirty years after the Convention, returning to the subject of monopolies and saying what the grant must be: temporary. The same passage warns that perpetual monopolies of every sort are forbidden by the genius of free government and by the imperfection of human foresight.
James Madison, “Monopolies. Perpetuities. Corporations. Ecclesiastical Endowments,” Detached Memoranda, ca. January 31, 1820. Founders Online, National Archives.
View source →
Endnote 3
Singer Mfg. Co. v. June Mfg. Co. (1896)
Where the Court’s line on expiry begins. On expiration, the right to make the invention passes to the public. The essay dates the rule from here, which is why it says “since 1896” rather than anything vaguer.
Singer Mfg. Co. v. June Mfg. Co., 163 U.S. 169, 185 (1896).
View source →
Scott Paper Co. v. Marcalus Mfg. Co. (1945)
Half a century later the Court added the words this essay leans on. What the public gains on expiry cannot be taken back, whatever the legal device employed. The phrase matters because it is not about patents alone; it is about devices. The Court quoted it again seventy years later in Kimble.
Scott Paper Co. v. Marcalus Mfg. Co., 326 U.S. 249, 256 (1945), quoted in Kimble v. Marvel Entertainment, LLC, 576 U.S. 446 (2015).
View source →
Endnote 4
“The exception proves the rule”
The maxim is not a joke about exceptions disproving rules. In its old legal sense, a stated exception implies a rule covering the cases not excepted. That is why aviation’s early carve-out matters to the essay: naming one exception in 1926 tells you what the rule was everywhere else. Holton’s paper traces the maxim and defends that reading.
Richard Holton, “The Exception Proves the Rule,” Journal of Political Philosophy 18, no. 4 (2010): 369–388. The essay also cites H. W. Fowler, A Dictionary of Modern English Usage, headword “exception,” and Francis Bacon, De augmentis scientiarum (1623), aphorism 17. Bacon’s aphorism was checked in the 1640 Watts translation as quoted; the Fowler edition and headword have not been independently verified.
View source →
Endnote 5
Bonito Boats v. Thunder Craft Boats (1989)
The Court states the default. Free exploitation of ideas is the rule; a federal patent is the exception to it. The opinion also treats novelty and nonobviousness as implicit in the Patent Clause itself, which places the limit in the Constitution rather than in the patent statute alone.
Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc., 489 U.S. 141, 151 (1989).
View source →
Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stiffel Co. (1964)
A pole lamp whose patents turned out to be invalid. The Court held that a state could not use its own unfair-competition law to forbid copying what federal patent law had left open to everyone. The case is about a state law, but the reasoning is about who owns an unpatented design: the public.
Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stiffel Co., 376 U.S. 225 (1964).
View source →
Kimble v. Marvel Entertainment (2015)
The modern restatement. A patent holder cannot collect royalties for use after the patent expires, and the Court declined to overrule Brulotte, the 1964 case that said so. Expiry is a hard line, not a negotiating position.
Kimble v. Marvel Entertainment, LLC, 576 U.S. 446 (2015).
View source →
Graham v. John Deere Co. (1966)
The Court reads the Patent Clause as two things at once: a grant of power to Congress and a limitation on it. Congress may not authorize patents whose effect is to remove existing knowledge from the public domain. That sentence is the essay’s hinge, because the essay asks what happens when a different federal power has that effect.
Graham v. John Deere Co., 383 U.S. 1, 5–6 (1966).
View source →
Endnote 6
Air Commerce Act of 1926
The start of the narrow exception. In 1926 the federal government took up the certification of aircraft designs, and it did so at the urging of the aviation industry itself. Aviation is the one place where a federal design approval stood between an ordinary builder and the market long before such rules reached cars, appliances or lighting.
Air Commerce Act of 1926, 44 Stat. 568, signed May 20, 1926. Federal Aviation Administration, “Origins of the FAA.”
View source →
The first type certificate, 1927
How quickly the new regime bit. Approved Type Certificate No. 1 went to the Buhl Airster on March 29, 1927, less than a year after the Act. From that point an aircraft design needed the government’s approval before anyone could build it for sale.
National Research Council, Improving the Continued Airworthiness of Civil Aircraft (1998), chapter 2. The web address reads “chapter/4” but the page shown is printed chapter 2.

Endnote 7
Type Certificate Data Sheet 3A12
The airplane’s paper trail in one document. The Model 172 was approved November 4, 1955; the 172R amendment is dated June 21, 1996 and the 172S May 1, 1998; the certificate transferred to Textron Aviation on July 29, 2015. The sheet covers the 172 series only. The Cessna 170 sits on a different certificate, A-799.
FAA Type Certificate Data Sheet 3A12. Search “3A12” in the FAA Dynamic Regulatory System. The viewer’s direct document links are not durable, so the search page is given instead.

How many were built
The scale of what one certificate has carried. Textron Aviation put deliveries of the Skyhawk at more than 45,000 airplanes.
Textron Aviation press release, July 21, 2026.

The landing-gear patents
The clock the essay runs on. Three patents cover the original spring-steel landing gear: two to Steve Wittman, U.S. 2,163,653 (1939) and U.S. 2,498,976 (1950), and U.S. 2,597,265, issued May 20, 1952 to Thomas B. Salter for Cessna. The last of them ran its seventeen-year term and expired on May 20, 1969. That date belongs to the original gear; the rest of the design followed as its own patents ran.
U.S. Patent No. 2,597,265 (Salter, May 20, 1952), linked below; U.S. Patent Nos. 2,163,653 and 2,498,976 (Wittman). Sources conflict on whether Cessna licensed Wittman’s gear or bought the rights outright, so the essay uses neither word.

Endnote 8
What the 172K through 172P were built to
The heart of the case. Through seventeen years of production after 1969, these models were certificated to Civil Air Regulations Part 3, which took effect November 1, 1949, as amended 3-1 through 3-12, with Part 36 noise rules and two 1978 marking requirements added. A rulebook written before 1965 governed airplanes rolling out of the factory in 1986.
FAA Type Certificate Data Sheet 3A12, certification basis for Models 172 through 172P, including 14 C.F.R. §§ 23.1559 and 23.1545(a) (1978). Search “3A12” in the FAA Dynamic Regulatory System.
Search “3A12” →
What the 1996 restart was built to
When production resumed, the airplane was not held to the current rulebook either. Its basis is Part 23 as amended 23-1 through 23-6, and amendment 23-6 took effect August 1, 1967, with several dozen later sections named one at a time and three findings of equivalent safety rather than compliance: induction system icing, throttle controls and mixture controls. The document that records all this is a 2005 notice about a diesel engine conversion, which restates the factory certification bases. That is why a diesel notice is cited for a 1996 fact.
FAA, Special Conditions No. 23-157-SC, 70 Fed. Reg. 2561, 2561–62 (January 14, 2005); equivalent-safety findings at 14 C.F.R. §§ 23.1093, 23.1143(g) and 23.1147(b).
View source →
The rules a competitor would have faced
What was piling up on the other side of the fence while the 172 kept its older basis. Part 23 was amended thirty-three times by 1986. These are not placards and lights that a builder can bolt on afterward; they reach into the design itself. Amendment 23-7 landed September 14, 1969; 23-15 rewrote fuel system requirements on October 31, 1974; 23-23 changed design and construction on December 1, 1978; 23-33 changed cockpit controls on August 11, 1986.
FAA, “Summary of Part 23 Amendments.”
View source →
The two rules that draw the line
Why the same airplane met two different rulebooks depending on who built it. Part 21, adopted October 24, 1964, holds a new type-certificate applicant to the rules in effect when the application is made, and allows a production certificate only to the holder of the type certificate or to someone with rights under its licensing agreement. Nobody had to aim a rule at the expired patent. These two sections did the work.
Part 21 as adopted October 24, 1964 (Docket No. 5085), § 21.17 and § 21.133(a), 29 Fed. Reg. 14569. The eligibility rule is now codified at 14 C.F.R. § 21.132, subpart G having been renumbered in 2009; § 21.17 keeps its number. Cited here to the current codification rather than to the 1964 section number, so that the link and the citation agree.

Endnote 9
General Aviation Revitalization Act of 1994
What ended the shutdown, and what it did not touch. The Act set an eighteen-year statute of repose on product liability claims, which is what brought light-airplane production back. It waived no airworthiness standard and gave nobody a certification advantage. The liability problem and the certification wall are separate things.
General Aviation Revitalization Act of 1994, Pub. L. No. 103-298, 108 Stat. 1552.
View source →
The restart, as the pilots’ press told it
Confirmation from outside the government record. The FAA first wanted the restarted airplane certificated to current rules, and the airplane was ultimately approved partly to older amendments. The negotiation happened, and the older basis survived it.
“Last of the Old vs. First of the New,” AOPA Pilot, June 2013.

Endnote 10
Sears at 231: who may build
The sentence the essay sets against the aviation record. An article on which the patent has expired is in the public domain, and may be made and sold by whoever chooses to do so. Same airframe lineage, same agency, different rule.
Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stiffel Co., 376 U.S. 225, 231 (1964). The printed opinion carries a comma after “domain”; Kimble quotes the sentence without one, and the essay follows Kimble.
View source →
Endnote 11
EPA Tier 1 emission standards
The rulebook a full-size pickup of 2000 through 2003 was actually built to. Trucks over 6,000 pounds gross weight stayed on Tier 1 until 2004, which is why the essay says full-size pickup: the lightest trucks had already moved to a different program.
EPA, Tier 1 Motor Vehicle Emission Standards, final rule, 56 Fed. Reg. 25724 (June 5, 1991).
View source →
EPA Tier 3 emission standards
The rulebook the same truck would have to meet if someone built it new today. The fleet-average target of 0.030 grams per mile of NMOG plus NOx is not reached until model year 2025, so the gap between the two rulebooks is not a rounding difference.
EPA, Tier 3 Motor Vehicle Emission and Fuel Standards, final rule, 79 Fed. Reg. 23414 (April 28, 2014).
View source →
FMVSS 138, tire pressure monitoring
One of the requirements added after the truck’s own model years. Tire pressure monitoring became mandatory with model year 2008.
NHTSA, Tire Pressure Monitoring Systems (FMVSS No. 138), final rule, 70 Fed. Reg. 18136 (April 8, 2005).

FMVSS 126, electronic stability control
A requirement that reaches into the design rather than sitting on top of it. Electronic stability control became mandatory with model year 2012.
NHTSA, Electronic Stability Control Systems (FMVSS No. 126), final rule, 72 Fed. Reg. 17236 (April 6, 2007).

FMVSS 214, the side-impact pole test
The requirement that changes the body structure itself, and the clearest example of a rule a faithful remake cannot simply bolt on.
NHTSA, Side Impact Protection pole test (FMVSS No. 214), final rule, 72 Fed. Reg. 51908 (September 11, 2007).

FMVSS 214, when it actually bit
The date the essay uses, and why it is later than the rule. After petitions, the pole test applied to all light vehicles from September 1, 2014, which is model year 2015.
NHTSA, response to petitions for reconsideration (FMVSS No. 214 phase-in), 73 Fed. Reg. 32473 (June 9, 2008).
