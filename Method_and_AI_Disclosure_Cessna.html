Jefferson’s Bargain & the 1969 Betrayal

A Note on Method and AI‑Assisted Authorship

How the forgotten half of the Patent Clause came into view, and how the 1969 Cessna case study came into focus, was researched, and was written up

Roleigh Martin

Posted 29 September 2026, to accompany the essay “Jefferson’s Bargain: When Patents Expire, Anyone May Build. The 1969 Betrayal.” Any references below have citations, sometimes with an endnote commentary, here.

Background

This note explains how the essay was researched and written, and with which artificial-intelligence tools, in full and without being asked. The essay's core claim comes from my forthcoming book, The Forgotten Half of the Patent Clause. The 1969 Cessna case study was new work, done for the essay; the book was updated alongside it, because the Cessna record shows that the proposed remedy has a historical root. The note also answers a prior question: how a retired data engineer came to be focused on two words in Article I, and on the half of the Patent Clause they protect, the public’s right to the expired-patent commons, which patent attorneys, environmental lawyers, regulatory economists, and constitutional scholars have spent fifty-six years ignoring.

The two words are underlined in the Constitution’s Article I, Section 8, Clause 8: “securing for limited Times to Authors and Inventors the exclusive Right”. This was written in 1787, two and a half years before the first patent law. These words are therefore a constitutional limit on securing exclusivity by any means, not merely a rule for patent statutes. A secured restriction against competition must expire. For 183 years no second federal power crossed that limit in ordinary commerce; the encroachments the Court struck down came from state law and private contract.

My essay covers a most notable area of commerce where a securing of protection for an invention continued after its patents expired prior to 1970, when the first part of the eventual regulatory wall was created with passage of the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1970 (Pub. L. 91-604) and signed on December 31, 1970. They're the foundation of the vehicle-emissions wall, which led to rules that keep faithful remakes of patent-expired vehicles off the market (and later some machines not part of vehicles). The exception case is aviation and this essay covers it. The last known patent behind the original Cessna 172 expired on May 20, 1969. This is U.S. Patent 2,597,265, Thomas B. Salter's refinement of the spring-steel landing gear, assigned to Cessna. It was issued May 20, 1952 and ran seventeen years from grant.

Who is asking you to read this

I am a co-inventor of U.S. Patent No. 5,359,509, an early artificial-intelligence system for adjudicating health-insurance claims, once archived in the permanent research collection of the Smithsonian Institution. I spent thirty-four years in the information-technology department of a Fortune 25 health insurer, troubleshooting complex code and designing databases, after a B.S. with minors in American and Russian literature and physical science and an M.A. in sociology. I am seventy-six, live in Northern Kentucky, and write full-time. On election day 2024 I created a Make American Living Affordable community on X, now more than 115,000 members, and my long-form articles there on the Patent Clause, Congressional history, related topics, and the cost of living have drawn more than eight million impressions.

Why was this the man who saw it? An eclectic and cross-disciplinary background, the kind a career counselor would call unfocused, is the specific cognitive preparation (what Thomas Kuhn called the outsider's vantage on a settled paradigm) needed to see across the field boundaries that specialists are trained to respect. That is a claim about standing rather than about conclusions, and the rest of this note puts evidence behind it.

How the “limited Times” clause came into view

The road ran through the cost of living. In March 2025 I published a short hard-science-fiction story set in an alternative universe where there is an America that had stopped adding new car rules after January 1, 1971. The article’s title is “Focus Tariffs & Deregulations (DOGE) on Cars: Make Cars Affordable Again”. One consequence stood out. A niche market emerges for low-cost cars built from patent-expired parts, the way electronic calculators went from pricey gadgets to cheap essentials once their patents ran out.

The freeze in that story was a thought experiment, and it taught me where the regulatory wall stands. The three federal laws in force by 1970, the 1965 emissions act, the 1966 safety act, and the 1970 Clean Air Act amendments, contain no clock.

The first two handed agencies open-ended power to set standards; the third ordered one fixed step, a 90 percent cut in tailpipe emissions from the 1970 and 1971 model years by 1975 and 1976, and nothing after it. What the three laws left in place was a target.

The treadmill came later, from the 1975 fuel-economy statute with its year-by-year increases, the 1977 and 1990 Clean Air amendments, the 2007 energy act, and the agencies' habit of revising every standard every few model years under the authority those two old statutes had given them. Both statutes attach their standards to a vehicle by its year of manufacture rather than its year of design, and that trigger is the whole blockage: every new standard becomes a bar on every old design. Freeze the standards and the trigger still fires, but it points at rules a 1970 platform already meets. A faithful remake of an expired design is lawful by being what it is, as it had been for the previous 183 years.

Congress could have told the agencies to honor the expired patent public domain, and the problem would never have arisen. Federal law already trusts that time-shift in aviation, where the stakes are highest, as the essay explains. The difference, as the Cessna record shows, is who may use it.

Then on May 18, 2025, I set out the spine of what became the book in my article “The Hidden War on Expired Patents-Regulations Drive Up $–Make American Living Affordable (MALA)!”. I reviewed in the article the "limited Times" constraint, Jefferson's 1813 letter to Isaac McPherson, the patent as a social contract rather than an inherent right, and the claim that a web of modern regulation now defeats the public's half of that bargain, with 1970 as the hinge.

That article disclosed, when it ran, that the research had been done partly with Grok. This note continues a practice I followed in public while the work was in progress.

What made "limited Times" arrest the eye is a habit from software. A compiler does not interpret intent. It reads what you wrote, applies the language's rules, and returns either an executable result or an error. That habit becomes how a person reads any structured text, and it is how I read Article I, Section 8, Clause 8: besides asking what the framers meant and what practice has settled into, I read the clause as a line of code. Limited Times has a type signature. It is a duration. A duration has a start and an end, and after the end the resource (the right to copy and remanufacture the invention) enters the public domain. None of that is interpretation. Constitutional lawyers are trained to find meaning in penumbras and emanations, and most constitutional questions need that training. A few are simple syntax. The Patent Clause, on what happens when the limited time ends, is one of them.

The paradox

My book is, paradoxically, an AI-assisted answer to the AI-assisted problem.

Americans' loudest complaint about technology in 2026 is that artificial intelligence is coming for their jobs. The book argues that restoring the right to remanufacture patent-expired durable goods (pickup trucks built on a 1990s Ford F-150 platform, tractors on a 1970-era John Deere platform, furnaces on a turn-of-the-century Carrier platform) lets a buyer who chooses the remanufactured good, as households in the lower forty percent are projected to do almost exclusively, pay half or less of the new price, while competition pulls down the price of new goods themselves by roughly fifteen percent. For truckers, whose work autonomous rigs now threaten, the remedy does not stop AI from being deployed in trucking. It changes the math underneath the deployment, so that the AI's labor-displacing advantage becomes the advantage of a tool rather than of a regulatory subsidy.

What makes the paradox acute is that none of the analysis behind that argument could have been done, by me alone at a kitchen table in Northern Kentucky, without the same tools whose downstream consequences the book is trying to mitigate.

I am not a constitutional lawyer. I am not a patent attorney. I have never been licensed to practice law, never clerked at a federal court, never taught at a law school. A case across Supreme Court doctrine of the kind the book makes would normally be assembled by a research team at a law-school clinic or a public-interest legal organization.

I assembled it instead, a retired data engineer with disciplined access to a small set of AI tools: Anthropic's Claude for prose drafting and structural editing; Abacus AI's DeepAgent for multi-step source retrieval; OpenCase, a legal model trained on Cornell's Legal Information Institute datasets of United States statutes and Supreme Court opinions and indexed daily against open federal and state opinion archives, used to surface candidate authorities and to argue against the thesis; X's SuperGrok for source material the other models cannot read; GPT-6 Astra and earlier, GPT-5 Thinking, as a cross-model auditor of Claude's output; Perplexity Pro for general research; Nano Banana Pro for visual production; and Meta AI, DeepSeek Pro, Microsoft Copilot, and Gab's Arya as additional cross-model auditors and as respondents to the open-book examination.

The essay itself drew on a narrower set: Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5 and 5.5 High, Claude Fable High, GPT-6 Astra, Grok, and OpenCase. The Cessna case study was where the new research lay.

Claude and OpenCase surfaced the Supreme Court authorities, and OpenCase was set to argue against the reading of them. Claude, Grok and GPT-6 Astra checked the aviation record: type certificate 3A12, Part 21 of the federal aviation regulations, and the certificate's transfer to Textron. It was Grok's check that showed today's factory 172S still carries patented equipment, which is why the case study rests on the pre-1986 Skyhawk, whose patents have all run. Grok, OpenCase and GPT-6 Astra and most important, human editing, audited the drafts and sent the Cessna paragraph through many dozens of edited versions before its wording held. Every citation any tool produced, for the book or the essay, was checked against the primary source before use.

What the law says about a book made this way

On January 29, 2025, the U.S. Copyright Office published Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability (copyright.gov/ai), concluding that works created by human authors using AI tools are eligible for copyright protection when the human provides substantial creative direction, selection, and arrangement. The Office confirmed that "the use of AI to assist in the process of creation or the inclusion of AI-generated material in a larger human-generated work does not bar copyrightability." All constitutional arguments, litigation strategy, savings methodology, editorial structure, and substantive conclusions in the book and the essay are my own original intellectual work; AI tools served as research assistants and drafting aids under my direction.

The standard the Office applied is not a formality. Copyright attaches to what a human contributes (the selection, the arrangement, the creative direction) and not to material a machine generated on its own. A prompt is not authorship. What is protected is the judgment exercised over the output. On that standard the question of whether the book is mine is not close, and the manuscript record answers it. As of September 22, 2026, the book manuscript stood at forty-two files carrying 1,166 saved revisions between them, an average of twenty-eight per file; the endnote apparatus alone was on its 219th version. A manuscript revised at that rate, for that long, is not the output of a prompt. It is the residue of a person deciding, several thousand times, what to keep, what to cut, what was wrong, and what had to be checked again.

The Cessna article itself, under 2,000 words long, has gone through 51 saved revisions.

A book and article assembled this way can be audited in a way a conventionally researched book cannot. Every authority is cited with a live link, in the book’s endnotes and the essay’s annotated bibliography. Every figure is either checked against a primary source or marked as unchecked. Appendix C of the book reproduces, unedited, the raw replies of eleven models to an open-book examination of the argument, a test of the reasoning by systems that had no part in producing it. A reader who distrusts the method has more to work with here, not less.

The Skilsaw

Most people use AI the way a child uses Lego bricks, snapping a few together and deciding it can't do anything sophisticated or new. Carpenters made the same misjudgment about power tools. The portable circular saw appeared in 1924, but hand labor ruled the job site until after World War II. Mark Erlich's oral history of the Massachusetts trade (1986) records what happened when the Skilsaw finally arrived in force. The older men resisted, and they had cause; early models carried marginal safety features. One carpenter hauled his Skilsaw to the roof and threw it off the building. "The saw's too fast," he said. "I'm going to cut by hand." He was judging a tool by its worst version.

An off-the-shelf chatbot is a fresh college graduate: quick, confident, and wrong often enough to cost you time.

Training it is the first job: settings files, project files, a writing guide, response modes, cross-validation against rival models, self-audits, agent deep dives, and the plain fact that a chatbot is not an agent. The advanced tiers run past a hundred dollars a month.

Training the machine is the smaller half.

Take a problem like the forgotten half of the Patent Clause. You have to be conversationalist, mentor, critic, debater, validator, and reader, often inside a single session. You have to bring your own library (history, law, engineering, economics) because the machine cannot tell you which discipline the answer is hiding in. You need a critical mind and the habit of thinking past the obvious. Linear logic checks the chain of reasoning. Lateral logic finds the chain worth checking. Without both, you will get fluent answers and never learn which ones were wrong. The Skilsaw did not make a man a carpenter. It made a good carpenter faster and a careless one dangerous.

Checkable, not certified

The book does not claim to have been independently verified. It claims to be checkable, which is a different property and a more useful one.

Every case, statute, and regulation it relies on is cited so that a reader can open it and see. A former federal energy regulatory attorney read successive drafts across a long correspondence and disagreed with parts of them; I rewrote the first chapter in consequence. Practicing attorneys are now engaging with the argument publicly, under their own names and with their reputations attached. That is not a certification that the argument is right. It is evidence that people consider it worth arguing with.

Artificial intelligence made this analysis possible for one person working alone.

Artificial intelligence is also, at this moment, putting fabricated legal citations in front of judges, and the failure has the same shape every time: somebody signed a citation nobody had read. The remedy is not to put the tool down. It is to read the citation. Every authority in the book was opened.

The tools were not used as ghost-writers. They were used as a small newsroom.

OpenCase, checking citations against the federal record, twice flagged circuit-court conflicts my synthesis had missed. GPT-5 Thinking audited Claude's drafts. Every claim of constitutional fact was vetted against a primary source I personally examined.

A word about what kind of author that makes me. Working this way, I was not so much a writer as a composer, editor, proof-reader, and conductor. A conductor plays none of the instruments, decides what is played, in what order, at what tempo, and what gets cut in rehearsal, and the performance is his.

That changes what the reader is being asked to do. A conventional book of ideas asks you to follow one man's thinking as it developed over a lifetime. This one asks you to ride along on a journey through two centuries of American history and 197 years of Supreme Court rulings on what happens when a patent expires: a journey nobody had taken before, and nobody could have taken alone. Not for want of intelligence; for want of years.

A language model can read the whole line of cases and every statute in the wall in the time it takes a person to read one brief. The reading path is longer than one working life. That is a large part of why the argument is new.

Reading the rules until they break

The constitutional question is a kind of problem (cross-disciplinary, hidden in plain sight, defended by overlapping institutional incentives) that the human alone has been unable to solve for fifty-six years. The closest analogy is security. Three cases from the last two years share one shape: a system defended by experts, a flaw the experts never found, and a machine that found it by reading the rules.

In March 2026 Microsoft published two critical advisories, each rated 9.8 on a scale of 10, for remote-code-execution flaws in Bing Images; both credit XBOW, an autonomous system that a year earlier had become the first non-human to top HackerOne's United States leaderboard of vulnerability researchers. OpenBSD, whose developers audit every line, carried a flaw in its internet-connection code for twenty-seven years that let a stranger crash the machine; a generation of careful programmers missed it, and in 2026 Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview found it. And in July 2025 Google saw signs that an attacker was preparing to use an unknown flaw in SQLite, the database engine in nearly every phone, browser, and car; Big Sleep, an AI agent built by DeepMind and Google's Project Zero, read the code and found the bug, and Google patched it first, the first time, Google said, that an artificial intelligence had foiled an exploit before it was used.

None of these systems was carelessly built. They were opened because a machine can hold every rule of a system in view at once and ask where the rules stop agreeing with each other. That is what the book does to a constitutional grant, the statutes written under it, and the regulations written under those: it reads them together and finds the place where they break. It is no coincidence that the year the book became writable is the year the tools became powerful enough to make it writable.

Security is the closest analogy, not the only record.

The book's last chapter, “When Outsiders Advance Constitutional Law and Other Disciplines”, collects a longer one: cases in which a machine found, in records a field already possessed, what the field's own experts had looked past.

In 2023 a physicist with no training in papyrology looked at scans of a carbonized Herculaneum scroll that the whole papyrological profession already had, looked longer than anyone else had, and noticed a faint cracked-mud texture that turned out to be ink, the first evidence of writing ever found inside an unopened scroll. A twenty-one-year-old undergraduate read his blog post, trained a model to find more of it, and recovered the Greek word for purple. Papyrologists then read the text and said whether it was Greek. In 2017 a software engineer put a neural network on the weak Kepler signals the professionals had reasonably skipped and surfaced an eighth planet around Kepler-90; an astronomer worked the problem with him, and the planet is listed in NASA's archive alongside every other confirmed world.

The medical cases take the operator's credentials out of the argument, because the operators were insiders working their own field's data. Doctors have photographed the back of the eye for about a hundred years; a system trained on retinal photographs from 284,335 patients estimated a patient's age from the picture alone, predicted whether the patient smoked and whether a major cardiac event was coming, and identified the patient's sex almost perfectly, which no ophthalmologist can do.

Breast cancer had been graded for decades by reading the tumor cells; a system called C-Path found that the ordinary connective tissue around the tumor predicted survival more strongly than the tumor did, on slides pathologists had been reading the whole time. A Mayo Clinic system read a weak-pumping heart off an ordinary electrocardiogram, a tracing recorded since the early 1900s, and proved it in a randomized trial of 22,641 adults across 120 primary-care teams.

In mathematics, AlphaTensor found a way to multiply certain small tables of numbers with fewer multiplications than the best method known since 1969, and within about a week two mathematicians, starting from the machine's output, went one step further on a small computer.

Two things run through every one of those cases, and both are the structure of this note. The machine was not told where to look; it read what the field already had and asked where the pattern was. And a human being decided what counted as proved: papyrologists read the Greek, an astronomer did the astronomy, Mayo ran the trial, mathematicians checked the proof. The instrument was new. The information was not.

That is the exact shape of the Cessna finding, my forthcoming book, and of the reading of 197 years of expiry rulings behind it: the records were public, the question had not been asked, and the machine could hold enough of the record at once to answer it. What counts as proved is still decided by people, and in this case that will be a court.

The search no one has run

If machines can read a system's rules and find where they break, the natural question is whether anyone has pointed one at the Constitution, the statutes passed under it, and the regulations written under those. I looked. No one has.

The nearest thing, the AI tool the U.S. DOGE Service built in 2025 to read roughly 200,000 federal regulations, asks a question one layer below this one. It asks whether a statute still requires a rule, not whether the statute and the rule, stacked together, do something the Constitution forbade.

Nobody has run the search downward from a constitutional clause, through the statutes and regulations built on it, to a live conflict no court has yet been asked about. That is the search the book runs, by hand and with the tools described above, on two words in Article I. The point of saying so is not priority. The method is available to anyone, the sources are public, and the field is empty. A reader who knows of an earlier such search should say so, and the correction will go in the next edition.

A word to editors

Most editors now treat AI-assisted writing the way the carpenter on the roof treated the Skilsaw: they have seen the worst version and made a rule against the tool. The worst version is real. An unsupervised model will produce fluent prose with citations that do not open, and a publisher who prints it deserves what follows.

But the rule protects against the wrong thing. It screens for the instrument when it should screen for the work.

The test that catches slop is older than the technology and takes an editor ten minutes: open the citations. A manuscript whose every authority resolves to a primary source, whose figures are marked checked or unchecked, and whose revision history shows a person deciding several thousand times what to keep and what to cut is not slop, whatever built the first draft. A manuscript that fails that test is slop, whether a machine or a graduate student wrote it. The Copyright Office already draws the line there, at the human judgment exercised over the output, and an editorial policy that draws it anywhere else is rejecting a method rather than judging a book.

That test catches the bad manuscript. It does not measure what the tool contributes to a good one. The problems America has failed to solve are, in the main, cross-disciplinary, and the book’s ability to help tackle a problem that is complex, large and crosses a multitude of disciplines and historical periods. Tackled well, it needs to consolidate its understanding of the problem and its proposed solution in a manner that is internally consistent; structurally sound; economically grounded and resistant to misinterpretation. Those four attributes are how Microsoft’s Copilot described my book’s core thesis.

The largest contribution the tools made to the book was structural. Seeing that the public's half of the Patent Clause had been withheld was the observation. Turning it into something a court can rule on is the work of Part V (“Restoring the Patent Bargain: Who Can Sue and How”), and that part could not have been assembled by one person without expert use of several engines at once. They helped identify the nineteen plaintiff categories, sovereign, public-fiduciary, direct, and associational, and my part was to push back hard at every one, questioning, thinking, and verifying until a category survived or fell. They mapped how each plaintiff can reach a court at minimal cost, now that a challenger no longer has to break a law to challenge one; how the same case can be filed in more than one circuit so that a conflict among the circuits makes it likely the Supreme Court takes the question; and how the people a plaintiff category represents can write to the associations able to carry the case for them, the playbook of Chapter 34 (“What You Can Do: A Citizen’s Playbook”). Any one of those pieces is a research project. Holding all of them in one frame, consistent with each other and with 197 years of holdings, is what the machines made possible.

I could not have done it alone. Nobody could.

The tools reviewed above cover whether the pieces click together. An argument that runs from a constitutional clause through 197 years of holdings, seventeen statutes, a wall of regulations, and a savings estimate for the lower forty percent of households has hundreds of joints, and a flaw at any one of them brings the rest down. One reproducible examination was done to test the thesis. See chapter 28, “Reason the Constitutional Argument Yourself: The Open-Book Examination”.

Gord Magill, author of End of the Road: Inside the War on Truckers, who wrote the foreword to the book and says he is no friend of AI content, describes what he found on reading it as an author who "acted as a maestro in directing high end AI engines, specifically built for case law and legal research." That is the standard. The question for an editor is not whether the author used the tool. It is how the author conducted and communicated it: chose the questions, set the machine on two centuries of holdings and a shelf of regulations, and checked what came back.

AI is one of the reasons American workers in 2026 feel the floor moving. AI is also the reason the book could be written at all. And editing and proof-reading the book is the reason this essay spun off of it.

If the argument succeeds, the households in the lower forty percent, who would buy newly remanufactured goods of twenty-year-old design almost to the exclusion of new ones, get back about half of what those goods now cost them.
